---
title: "What Threat Modeling Is and When to Apply It"
description: "Basic definition and the right moment to apply it"
---

Threat modeling is a structured process for identifying in advance how a system could be attacked, analyzing its architecture, its data flows and the points where it interacts with users or external components, with the goal of finding and mitigating risks before the system is built, not after an incident reveals them. Unlike a penetration test, which evaluates an existing system looking for exploitable vulnerabilities, threat modeling is ideally done during design, when it's still possible to change architectural decisions at low cost, and it focuses on thinking systematically about the attacker, their motivations and their capabilities, rather than on finding concrete technical flaws already present. Applying it makes the most sense at specific moments: when designing a new feature that handles sensitive data, when introducing a significant architectural change such as a new external integration, or when the product's risk profile changes, for example when moving from internal use to public exposure. An important nuance is that not every system requires the same level of rigor; a light, quick exercise for a low-risk feature provides more real value than applying the same exhaustive process to everything equally, which usually leads to fatigue and abandonment of the process.
